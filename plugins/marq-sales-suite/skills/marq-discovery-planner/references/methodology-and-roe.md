# Discovery methodology and rules of engagement

## Authority status

Marq's discovery source material documents a five-step framework:

1. Identify and validate the business problem.
2. Understand the current state.
3. Find the true root cause.
4. Explore consequences and negative ramifications.
5. Understand desired outcomes and the buying process.

The available source material does not establish that Marq formally or exclusively uses SPICED. The existing `spiced-call-coach` skill establishes SPICED as a coaching lens for completed calls, but that alone is not proof of an official company-wide methodology mandate.

Use the five-step sequence as the source-grounded discovery process. Use SPICED as a coverage taxonomy and shared language between pre-call planning and post-call coaching. Do not claim that SPICED is Marq's official methodology unless an authoritative current enablement source explicitly confirms it.

The five-step framework remains the best source-backed discovery guide, but its authority is limited: the material gives useful prompts and structure, not stage gates, call-type standards, or evidence that every first call must complete all five steps. Treat the adaptive prioritization below as operating guidance derived from the source framework, deal-acceleration guidance, and generalized call patterns.

## Five-step to SPICED mapping

| Five-step framework | Primary SPICED coverage | Important distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Business problem | Pain | A broad problem statement is not yet a validated pain with priority. |
| Current state | Situation, Pain | Capture workflow, actors, tools, frequency, scale, and what breaks. |
| Root cause | Pain, Situation | Separate symptoms from process, governance, data, tooling, or adoption causes. |
| Consequences | Impact | Use buyer-owned effects and metrics; never calculate ROI from public estimates as fact. |
| Desired outcome and buying process | Critical Event, Decision, plus desired future state | SPICED's Critical Event and Decision deserve separate treatment; desired outcome is not fully represented by either. |

The mapping is many-to-many. Do not mechanically label every question with one acronym or assume coverage equals qualification.

## Initial-call minimums

Aim to learn only what is needed to make the next honest decision. On a typical first conversation, prioritize:

- Why the conversation exists now, including source and buyer state.
- The current workflow or use case in the buyer's own words.
- A meaningful problem, constraint, or desired improvement—not merely interest in features.
- Who experiences or owns the problem.
- At least one credible consequence or reason the status quo matters.
- Enough timing and decision context to choose a next step or a no-fit exit.
- Mutual confirmation of the next decision and who should participate.

It is acceptable for quantified impact, economic buyer access, complete stakeholder mapping, detailed decision criteria, procurement, security, implementation, budget, and a formal critical event to remain open after an initial call. Promote any of these earlier when they determine basic fit or when the buyer is already in an active evaluation.

## What can wait

Unless the call type or buyer state demands it, defer:

- Exhaustive ROI modeling before the buyer validates the inputs.
- Full technical/security review before a relevant workflow is established.
- Procurement mechanics before there is plausible business sponsorship.
- Every stakeholder role before the affected workflow owner and next-decision participants are known.
- Detailed implementation design before the buyer agrees the future state is worth pursuing.
- Competitive teardown before understanding the buyer's evaluation approach.

## Adapt by call type

### Inbound

- Buyer state is often active but not always qualified.
- Establish what prompted the inquiry, current approach, desired outcome, urgency source, and evaluation path.
- Do not mistake a demo request for permission to skip discovery.
- A short contextual product view is acceptable after the buyer's priority is clear.

### Outbound

- Buyer state may be latent or unknown.
- The first job is to earn relevance and test whether the problem pattern exists.
- Use a transparent hypothesis and allow easy disconfirmation.
- Avoid budget and full buying-process interrogation until the buyer validates relevance.
- A valid exit is permission for a deeper workflow conversation with the right owner; another valid exit is no priority and no follow-up.

### Partner

- Qualify bilateral value, target customer/use case, ownership boundaries, technical feasibility, commercial model, enablement burden, and the next joint experiment.
- Distinguish a product integration from a go-to-market partnership. One does not prove the other.
- Request additional stakeholders when the next decision depends on product, alliances, legal, or commercial ownership.

### Expansion

- Treat usage and support data as signals, then ask the customer to interpret them.
- Establish current value first, then the adjacent workflow, users, gap, incremental impact, adoption readiness, and sponsor.
- Do not imply low usage proves dissatisfaction or high usage proves expansion intent.
- The exit should specify the expansion hypothesis, cohort/use case, success evidence, and owner.

### Executive-led

- Lead with strategic context, business outcomes, cross-functional ownership, risk, and the decision to enable.
- Keep operational questions few; use them to locate the right follow-on owners.
- Request stakeholder expansion when the executive validates importance but delegates process knowledge or implementation.
- Do not spend executive time on a broad feature tour.

## Buyer state and maturity

- **Latent:** determine whether a normalized problem is important enough to examine. Use a brief relevant pattern or proof point, then pass the conversation back to the buyer.
- **Active:** understand the trigger, evaluation criteria, alternatives, timeline, and what is missing from the current approach.
- **Discovery stage:** deepen problem, current state, root cause, impact, and affected stakeholders.
- **Solution validation:** confirm the diagnosis, buying criteria, proof required, and narrow demo/test plan.
- **Decision process:** clarify approval path, business case, risks, decision owners, and critical event.
- **Final negotiation:** discovery should focus only on newly surfaced blockers or changed assumptions; use the deal execution workflow for the rest.

## Rules of engagement

### Objective

Define the business decision the conversation should enable, not an activity such as "show Marq" or "ask discovery questions."

### Agenda

Use three or four conversational moves:

1. Confirm why the meeting was scheduled and what would make it useful to the buyer.
2. Understand the relevant workflow, problem, and consequence.
3. Explore decision context and, only if earned, connect a narrow Marq capability or proof.
4. Decide together what happens next.

### Upfront contract

A good opening confirms time, gives the buyer a chance to modify the agenda, and names the end decision. Example pattern: "We have 30 minutes. I thought we'd first understand what prompted this and how the workflow works today, then—if there's a meaningful fit—spend a few minutes on the most relevant part of Marq. At the end, let's decide whether a deeper session with the right people makes sense. What would you change?"

Do not make a memorized script sound like mutual agreement. If time changes, re-contract immediately.

### Target exit decision

Choose one decision, not a vague "next steps" outcome. Examples:

- Proceed to a tailored solution-validation session around named workflows.
- Bring in the workflow owner, economic sponsor, technical evaluator, or affected user group for a defined reason.
- Validate a partner use case with one joint sample and named product owners.
- Hold the opportunity until a defined trigger/date.
- Close as not qualified because need, maturity, budget range, technical fit, or ownership is absent.

### Duration

No repository source establishes a universal Marq call length. Use the scheduled duration as authoritative. Design defaults, not policy:

- 20–30 minutes: first discovery or executive alignment; six core questions is usually enough.
- 30–45 minutes: standard discovery or mixed demo/discovery; six to eight questions.
- 45–60 minutes: multi-stakeholder workflow discovery or buyer-requested tailored validation; still prioritize rather than expanding into a questionnaire.

### Demo posture

Demo when the buyer has validated a relevant workflow/problem and a short product view will help make the target decision. Keep it tied to one or two use cases and return to discovery after buyer reactions.

Do not demo when the buyer's reason for meeting, use case, affected users, or desired change is still unclear; when the seller is relying only on a public hypothesis; or when the attendees cannot evaluate the workflow being shown. A buyer-requested demo may begin with a brief orientation, but first contract for enough discovery to tailor it.

### Additional stakeholders

Request stakeholders when their participation materially improves the next decision, including:

- The process owner when the current contact cannot describe the workflow.
- The creative/brand owner when governance and templates are central.
- A sales, field, campus, franchise, or local user when adoption and distributed creation matter.
- IT/security/data owners when integration feasibility is the next gate.
- An economic sponsor when impact is credible but funding/priority is unclear.
- Product/alliance/commercial owners for partner decisions.

Name `Who`, `What`, and `Why`. Do not ask for executive access as a generic stage requirement.

### Acceptable next steps

A next step is acceptable only when it has a specific action, participants, purpose, timing or trigger, and owner. Sending material alone is not deal progress unless it enables a named buyer action and follow-up decision.
