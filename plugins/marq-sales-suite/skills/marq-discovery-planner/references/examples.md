# Worked examples and manager commentary

These generalized examples teach judgment, not scripts. They contain no customer identities, private links, transcript excerpts, deal data, prices, or customer-specific commitments. Do not copy example facts or assumptions into a live account. The call reviews describe strong moments and important limitations; they are not endorsements of every seller behavior.

## Call plan 1: inbound higher-education creative leader

### Context

- Scheduled 30-minute first conversation after an SDR call.
- Public signal: recent institutional merger and rebrand.
- Buyer-stated in SDR notes: thousands of departmental logos and many distributed designers.
- Unknown: whether the current process is failing; the prospect has said collaboration is working well.

### Plan

- **Objective:** determine whether the rebrand creates a material governed-self-service problem worth a tailored workflow session.
- **Target exit:** if a recurring workflow with meaningful impact exists, schedule a session with the creative/brand owner around that workflow; otherwise close or nurture until post-rebrand demand is visible.
- **Questions:**
  1. What would make this conversation useful given everything underway with the rebrand? `Objective: buyer priority and permission.`
  2. How are requests for departmental and program materials handled today? `Objective: current workflow and actors.`
  3. Which request types repeat often enough that you would not want designers rebuilding them each time? `Objective: recurring use case.`
  4. Where does the current collaboration work especially well, and where—if anywhere—does it still create delay or risk? `Objective: avoid assuming pain.`
  5. When someone outside the core team needs a variation, what can they change and what must stay controlled? `Objective: governance/flexibility boundary.`
  6. What is the consequence if this stays exactly as it is through the next phase of the rebrand? `Objective: impact and priority.`
  7. Who would need to judge whether a templated workflow is useful and adoptable? `Objective: next-decision stakeholders.`
- **Conditional proof:** Western Colorado University only after the buyer validates repeated small requests; Marq permissions/brand kits only after the buyer explains brand-control needs.
- **Risk:** turning the merger into presumed pain or rushing into a general demo.

### Manager commentary

This plan respects an unusual but important answer: the buyer may genuinely have enough design capacity and a process that works. The best question is not "How bad is brand inconsistency?" but where the current model works and where it does not. The letterhead or department-logo workflow can become the concrete wedge; the public rebrand is only context.

## Call plan 2: partner-led integration and go-to-market discovery

### Context

- 30-minute follow-up with a DAM/PIM integration partner, product leader, and commercial leader.
- Buyer-stated: a working connector exists and product-spec-sheet automation may be a better use case than the initial pharmaceutical example.
- Unknown: repeatable customer demand, joint value, ownership, commercial model, and the smallest credible market test.

### Plan

- **Objective:** choose one joint use case and decide whether to invest in a repeatable customer-validation experiment.
- **Target exit:** name one target account/use case, sample asset/data flow, owners, and review date—or stop until demand evidence exists.
- **Questions:**
  1. What did the prototype prove technically, and what remains unproven? `Objective: evidence boundary.`
  2. Which customer workflow has the strongest observed demand rather than theoretical fit? `Objective: market evidence.`
  3. Who owns the source data, template, approval, and final asset in that workflow? `Objective: system/role boundaries.`
  4. What measurable work would the combined solution remove or improve? `Objective: buyer value.`
  5. Which customer could validate this with the least custom work? `Objective: test candidate.`
  6. What would each company need to provide for a credible sample? `Objective: effort and ownership.`
  7. If the sample works, what commercial motion fits—referral, co-sell, or something else—and who decides? `Objective: decision path.`
  8. What result and date tell us to continue or stop? `Objective: mutual exit criteria.`
- **Conditional proof:** current Marq developer documentation for templates, smart fields, API-based project creation, and approvals.
- **Risk:** mistaking a functional connector for partner-market fit.

### Manager commentary

Partner calls drift easily into technical admiration. This plan forces separate decisions about technical feasibility and go-to-market value. The right next step is not "tighten the demo" in the abstract; it is a named test with a customer pattern, evidence threshold, owner, and stop condition.

## Call plan 3: outbound/early inbound with low user maturity

### Context

- 30-minute first call with a manufacturer that wants free, customizable collateral for hundreds of small downstream businesses.
- Buyer-stated: downstream users resist accounts and primarily use editable PDFs and basic office tools.
- Unknown: adoption appetite and budget. The proposed value is indirect and the manufacturer is not yet seeing user demand.

### Plan

- **Objective:** determine whether the workflow, user maturity, and economic value can support an enterprise platform now.
- **Target exit:** qualify only if there is a credible adoption cohort, funded owner, and value commensurate with the model; otherwise close as not now and define a future trigger.
- **Questions:**
  1. What response have you seen from downstream businesses to the editable materials already available? `Objective: demonstrated demand.`
  2. How many have asked for image customization or another capability in the last six months? `Objective: frequency/scale.`
  3. What is the simplest workflow those users will reliably complete? `Objective: adoption ceiling.`
  4. Who would administer accounts, templates, and support? `Objective: operating model.`
  5. How would improving this program affect your business, beyond offering a nicer resource? `Objective: economic impact.`
  6. Is there an approved investment range or is this still an experiment? `Objective: commercial maturity.`
  7. What would need to be true for a platform investment to make sense? `Objective: future trigger/no-fit exit.`
- **Demo posture:** no broad demo. Show one form-to-collateral flow only if the account/login and adoption model are viable.
- **Risk:** falling in love with functional fit while ignoring commercial and adoption fit.

### Manager commentary

This is where disciplined discovery protects both sides. A product can solve the requested task and still be wrong for the buyer's maturity, budget, and end-user behavior. A respectful `not now` with a trigger is a successful outcome.

## Call review 1: higher-education rebrand conversation

**Pattern:** generalized higher-education conversation with a university creative-services leader.

### Strong moments

- The seller checked the remaining time after the prospect arrived late.
- The prospect directly challenged the value and explained that collaboration was currently working well.
- The seller adapted to concrete questions about permissions, brand kits, departmental logos, and letterhead automation.
- A specific repeatable use case emerged from the buyer rather than from public research.

### Why it was useful

The buyer's early "I am not sure we need this" was the most valuable discovery moment. The eventual letterhead use case was much stronger than generic higher-ed pain because it was buyer-owned and tied to the live rebrand.

### What a manager should still change

The seller moved into a platform tour before establishing the consequence, scale of repeat work, decision ownership, or what would make change worthwhile. The next plan should reopen those gaps rather than assume product interest equals qualification. The proper exit at that point was a conditional internal buyer review, not a qualified opportunity.

## Call review 2: DAM/PIM partner conversation

**Pattern:** generalized partner demo/discovery with a DAM and PIM integration firm.

### Strong moments

- The partner openly said the original use case was weak and proposed a more credible PIM/spec-sheet workflow.
- The conversation surfaced actual customer examples, manual data-to-document work, the technical data flow, and approval behavior.
- Both sides distinguished referral from reseller motions and discussed why the partner preferred a lighter commercial model.
- The meeting ended with mutual interest in improving the use-case demo and continuing the commercial conversation.

### Why it was useful

The best discovery came from letting the partner reinterpret the product through its own customer base. The seller listened, reflected the spec-sheet automation opportunity, and did not force the original pharmaceutical narrative.

### What a manager should still change

The exit remained too broad. A stronger conclusion would name one PIM, one customer pattern, one sample spec sheet, owners on both sides, success criteria, and a date. Technical excitement is not demand evidence.

## Call review 3: correct outcome was not qualified now

**Pattern:** generalized discovery conversation with a manufacturer serving many low-technology downstream locations.

### Strong moments

- The buyer explained the industry, downstream workflow, existing editable-PDF approach, desired image upload, user sophistication, and aversion to logins.
- The seller clarified that end users would need accounts and explored whether accounts could be created for them.
- Commercial reality was discussed directly: the buyer expected lightweight form-tool economics, had not validated downstream demand, and said the enterprise price was a no-go.
- Both sides acknowledged that Marq's functionality fit but the current maturity, adoption model, and economics did not.

### Why `not qualified now` was correct

The buyer had a real use case but no demonstrated adoption appetite, no enterprise investment case, and a strong requirement for near-zero user friction. Continuing as though feature fit overcame those constraints would waste both sides' time. The appropriate exit was to close or nurture until a named trigger such as validated user demand, a funded program, or a materially different delivery model.

### What a manager should still change

The seller could have reached the commercial/adoption conclusion earlier by asking about downstream demand, acceptable user behavior, administration ownership, and investment range before a long demo. After the buyer clearly disqualified the current model, the seller should avoid creating false hope about exceptional pricing unless an approved alternative actually exists.

## Reusable judgment

- Strong discovery is not measured by question count or product enthusiasm.
- A concrete buyer-owned workflow beats an industry pain narrative.
- Functional fit, operational fit, commercial fit, and timing are separate tests.
- A tailored demo is earned by relevance; it does not create relevance on its own.
- A close/no-fit decision can be the most buyer-respectful and commercially productive outcome.
